Ask a Haitian child what language she dreams in, and the answer is almost always Kreyòl. Ninety five percent of Haitians speak it. At most one in ten speaks French.

Yet for generations, Haitian children were taught to read in French, often by rote, in a language most of them never spoke at home. Children who struggled did not fall behind because they could not learn. They fell behind because the first door to reading was locked.

## What the research says

A child's brain builds itself most intensively from birth to age four. The words a child hears at home in those years become the foundation for reading. Children learn to read best in the language they already speak, then carry those skills into a second language. That is why PILH's approach is simple: Kreyòl first, and French as the bridge in school.

## What we found

When PILH walked through 32 classrooms in six rural schools, we found not one storybook and not one bookshelf. There were no alphabet charts either. So we made our own.

Six Kreyòl easy readers, co published with Haitian illustrators and translators and edited page by page by Haitian linguists. The Alfabè Kreyòl Mwen alphabet chart, thirteen years in the making with MIT linguists, now in 98 schools and reaching 3,800 students.

## Joy first

A first book should be a pleasure, not a test. Children laugh at Serafina the donkey, cheer for Jesula as she learns to read, and learn the story of Haitian independence through Malachi's soup joumou. They read these stories, then retell them to each other.
