## The window that matters most

A child's brain builds itself most intensively from birth to age four. Libraries can equip village teachers, parents and caregivers with that knowledge, and with the books that turn it into daily practice. When the youngest, most marginalized learners can freely reach books in their own language, they grow in confidence and become the strong readers a nation needs.

The joy has to come first, and it has to come in Kreyòl, with books that teach, delight and challenge.

## Book deserts

Haiti is the least literate country in the Western Hemisphere, with about fifty percent adult illiteracy. Inaccessibility to books is a major cause. During visits to a sample of six rural schools we found what can only be called book deserts. In thirty two classrooms there was not one extra book, not one bookshelf, and no source of books anywhere nearby.

Haiti's villages are scattered across mountains and hills, and that isolation cuts children off from age appropriate books and a culture of reading. About seventy percent of primary schools are private, mostly rural, and they rarely budget for pleasure or informational reading. Where parents provide for a family on less than 2.15 dollars a day, buying books is out of reach.

A library or book corner nearby changes the paradigm. It promotes reading, offers diverse books, gives young readers choices, and builds the habit of reading that fuels lifelong curiosity.

## Why Kreyòl

Kreyòl is the spoken language of ninety five percent of Haiti's eleven and a half million people. In February 2022 the Minister of Education proclaimed that public schools should teach children aged three to eight in Kreyòl. The previous standard was colonial French, often taught by rote, which frustrated struggling readers and helped drive seventy percent of Haitian children out of school before sixth grade.

Every PILH book, chart and training is built on a simple premise the government now shares: a Haitian child should learn to read in the Haitian language.
